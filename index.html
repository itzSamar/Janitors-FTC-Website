The game

BIOBUZZ is the 2026–27 FTC game. Launch balls into the Hive to tip it, unlock the Flowers at the one-minute mark, and own the most before the buzzer. Here is how it works, how it scores, and where our season stands.

The field.

The field is a 144-inch square of foam tile: one Hive at center, four Flowers on the walls. Four places to score, each worth a different amount.

The hive
20
per tip, auto or teleop

Two cells on a pivot, with one facing up. Launch balls into the upward cell until it flips. A tip is worth 20 points, ten times more than anything else, and it’s the only way to release more of your Nectar.

The flowers
2
per ball, if you own it

Four posts on the walls. They stay locked until 1:00 is left. If you own one, you get 2 points for every ball in it, even balls your opponent scored. Your lowest Nectar in a Flower adds 5 more.

The gardens
1
per ball

A taped area in two opposite corners. Each ball inside scores 1 point for that Garden’s color, no matter who put it there. Gardens aren’t protected, so the other alliance can drive over and take balls out.

The loading zones
5
per robot, parked

A 23 by 11 inch zone next to your alliance area. End at least partly inside it for 5 points, in auto and again in teleop. In auto, you also get 3 points just for driving off the wall.

How you score.

Every point value in BIOBUZZ, from table 10-2. One tip is worth ten balls in a Flower, so build for tips.

Swerve, from scratch.

A swerve drivetrain has four modules, and each one drives and steers on its own. That lets the robot move in any direction without turning first, while keeping full grip. Most rookie teams pick the simpler tank or mecanum. We designed and built all four modules ourselves.

DriveFour independently-steered swerve modules we designed ourselves.
ModulesBuilt and turning. The rest of the robot is built around them.
OdometryIn progress — dead-wheel tracking for field-centric control.
CodePlanned — we intend to write the swerve code ourselves.

The pros and cons of swerve.

There are three common drivetrains. Swerve is the hardest to build but the best to drive, so that’s the one we picked.

OptionTankMecanumSwerve
Moves sideways
Traction while pushedfullslipsfull

Four modules also means four times as many things that can break, and custom parts we can’t just buy a spare of. So we built all four from the same drawing, keep spares of the parts most likely to fail, and have a mecanum drivetrain as backup.

Swerve drivetrain, v1.

four modules, built and turning

There are three common drivetrains, and we picked the hardest one. Tank drive is simple but can’t move sideways. Mecanum slips when it gets pushed. Swerve keeps full grip while pointing any direction, so we can drive out of a pin instead of getting stuck.

The tradeoff is four modules to build, four to tune, and code that controls all of them at once. We decided it was worth it.

Swerve drives in any direction.

Then we cut it and built it ourselves, from the belly plate up.

Next, we tune all four modules so they work together, then add field-oriented drive. None of this has been tested in a match yet.

Questions.

Where does the money actually go?

Parts, entry fees, and travel. A season costs us $10,000 to $12,500. About half goes to the robot, about a quarter to event registration, and the rest to tools, field pieces, and getting six people and a robot to events. Our spending is public on our Hack Club donation page.

Is a donation tax deductible?

Our money is held by Hack Club Bank, which is run through The Hack Foundation, a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. Donations through our donation page go through them, and you get a receipt. Check with your accountant before claiming it, and ask us if you need the EIN.

What do we get for sponsoring?

It depends on the amount. Every tier puts your logo on our site and hoodies. At $500 and up, it also goes on the robot, so it’s seen at every gym, library, and classroom we visit. Title partners are named in our team materials and get the biggest spot on the robot and pit banner.

Can we donate materials instead of money?

Yes, and sometimes that helps even more. Aluminum, fasteners, 3D printer filament, machine time, and shipping credit all go straight into the build. Tell us what you have, and we’ll tell you honestly if we can use it.

You are a rookie team. How do we know you will compete?

Fair question. The team is new, but most of us aren’t. Our members have competed in FTC, FRC on team 7419, and VEX V5. We’re registered for the 2026/27 BIOBUZZ season with six events planned, our drivetrain is built and turning, and we’ve already raised over $7,000. Our first meet is in October 2026.

What is FTC, and what is BIOBUZZ?

FIRST Tech Challenge is a robotics competition for students in grades 7 through 12. Every year, FIRST releases a new game, and teams design, build, and program a robot to play it. BIOBUZZ is the 2026/27 game. Our season page explains how it scores.

How do we join the team?

Email us. We meet in Dublin, CA, and we’re looking for people who want to build, program, or help with outreach. You don’t need experience, and we’ll teach you the tools. Tell us what you’re interested in, and we’ll get you to a meeting.
